You've built a decent client list on ProZ or TranslatorsCafe, an agency in Germany sends you a 2,000 EUR project, and then comes the question: "Please send an invoice with your VAT number." You live in Poland, you don't have a company, and registering a sole proprietorship (JDG) means ZUS contributions, a Polish accountant, and VAT filings in a system you barely know. The alternative: a business incubator that handles invoicing, VAT, and payroll so you can focus on translating.

## Why translators hit a wall with foreign clients

Translation agencies – especially in the EU – work on a B2B basis. They expect an invoice, usually with a VAT number, before they release payment. Direct clients found through ProZ, TranslatorsCafe, or LinkedIn often have the same expectation. Without a registered business entity, you can't issue a VAT invoice, and without a VAT invoice, you don't get paid.

Some translators try to work "informally" – receiving money into a personal account and hoping nobody asks questions. This creates two problems: you have no legal proof of income (critical for [residence permits](/blog/business-incubator-poland-residence-permit)), and the tax office may eventually come knocking.

## The business incubator solution

A business incubator (inkubator przedsiębiorczości) is a foundation that lends you its legal entity. It is VAT-registered with an EU VAT number (VAT-EU), holds multi-currency bank accounts, and can invoice any client in the world on your behalf. Here's the flow:

1. **You sign an agreement** with the incubator – one-time, electronically.
2. **You do the translation work** for your client.
3. **You report the job** – client name, amount, currency, period.
4. **The incubator issues the invoice** to your client within one business day.
5. **The client pays the incubator's bank account** in EUR, USD, GBP, or PLN.
6. **The incubator signs a civil-law contract with you** (umowa o dzieło for translations), deducts tax and its fee, and pays you out.

One incubator agreement covers every client – a German agency, a UK law firm, a US startup that found you on ProZ. Each gets its own invoice.

## VAT: EU clients vs non-EU clients

This is where most translators get lost, and where the incubator earns its fee.

**EU clients (Germany, France, Netherlands, etc.):** The invoice is issued with the incubator's VAT-EU number, and VAT is accounted for under the **reverse-charge mechanism**. That means 0% Polish VAT on the invoice. The client self-accounts for VAT in their country. You don't touch VAT at all.

**Non-EU clients (UK post-Brexit, USA, Canada, Australia):** The invoice is marked "not subject to Polish VAT" (export of services). Again, 0% VAT. The client pays the net amount.

**Polish clients:** Standard 23% VAT applies, charged by the incubator. This is relevant if you also work for Polish agencies or law firms – the same incubator handles both domestic and foreign invoicing.

You personally never register for VAT, never file a VAT return, never deal with JPK_V7 files. The incubator handles all of that.

## Multi-currency accounts and payment methods

The incubator maintains dedicated accounts in EUR, USD, and GBP. This matters because:

- **No conversion loss on receipt.** When an agency in Frankfurt pays 1,500 EUR, it arrives in a EUR account – no forced PLN conversion at a bad bank rate.
- **PayPal and Payoneer.** Some platforms and direct clients pay through PayPal or Payoneer. The incubator can receive these transfers too.
- **Your payout** is in PLN to your Polish bank account, converted at the NBP (National Bank of Poland) rate on the day of the internal settlement.

## The contract: umowa o dzieło with 50% copyright costs

Translation is a creative, copyright-protected work. Under Polish law, when you transfer copyright to a client (which you do with every delivered translation), the work qualifies for a **contract for specific work (umowa o dzieło)** with **50% deductible costs**.

What that means in practice:

- Only **50% of your gross income** is treated as taxable income.
- The effective income tax rate is roughly **6%** (12% tax on half the income).
- **No ZUS contributions** – umowa o dzieło carries no social security obligations (unless it's with your own employer, which doesn't apply here).

This is the single biggest advantage for translators using an incubator. Compare it to a JDG where you'd pay flat tax of 12% on the full profit plus ~1,900 PLN/month in ZUS.

<Note>
The 50% copyright costs are capped at 120,000 PLN of gross income per year. If you earn more, the excess is taxed at standard rates. For most freelance translators, this cap is more than enough.
</Note>

## Sworn translations and conference interpreting

**Sworn translators (tłumacz przysięgły):** If you hold a Polish sworn-translator certificate, you can still use an incubator for your non-sworn commercial work. Sworn translations that require your personal stamp and registry entry are a separate matter – those are performed in your capacity as a certified translator and are typically invoiced differently. The incubator can handle the commercial side while you manage the official side independently.

**Conference interpreting:** Simultaneous and consecutive interpreting for conferences, courts, or business meetings also qualifies for umowa o dzieło with 50% costs, as long as the work involves a creative, individual contribution. The incubator invoices the event organiser or agency, and you get the same tax treatment as for written translations.
